AI History

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and ML and relate them to modern AI systems.

The foundational era of Artificial Intelligence began in the 1950s with Alan Turing’s question of whether machines could think, leading to the 1956 Dartmouth Workshop where the term "Artificial Intelligence" was officially coined. Early breakthroughs focused on hard-coded, rule-based systems and symbolic logic, exemplified by IBM’s Deep Blue defeating chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997. These early systems relied heavily on human programmers to explicitly define every rule. Today, this deterministic logic survives as the structural backbone of modern AI, manifesting in everyday technology like Google Maps routing algorithms and decision-making systems used in banking and compliance software.

The paradigm shifted dramatically with the rise of Machine Learning (ML) and the Deep Learning Revolution of the 2010s, which moved away from rigid rules toward pattern recognition. A pivotal catalyst was the 2012 ImageNet competition, where AlexNet demonstrated the power of deep neural networks. This breakthrough directly powers modern computer vision applications such as Apple Face ID, Tesla vehicle perception systems, and Netflix recommendation engines.

The modern era of AI is defined by generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs), sparked by the 2017 introduction of the Transformer architecture. Transformers introduced the concept of self-attention, enabling systems to process language more effectively and understand context. This innovation powers tools such as ChatGPT and Google Gemini, allowing AI systems to generate text, assist with coding, and support creative and analytical tasks.

AI Ethics

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

Algorithmic bias and discrimination represent one of the most pressing ethical challenges in artificial intelligence and machine learning, as these systems inherently reflect and amplify the prejudices present in their training data. When historical data containing human biases is fed into predictive models, the AI perpetuates those inequalities under the guise of objective, mathematical neutrality. A stark societal implication of this is the entrenchment of systemic racism and sexism in critical life areas. For example, predictive policing algorithms have been shown to disproportionately target minority neighborhoods, while automated hiring tools have actively deprioritized female applicants by analyzing resumes based on historical corporate demographics that favored men.

The erosion of privacy and data autonomy constitutes another severe ethical dilemma, driven by AI's insatiable demand for massive datasets to train and improve its networks. Machine learning systems frequently scrape, aggregate, and analyze deeply personal data without explicit or informed consent, blurring the lines of digital surveillance and individual liberty. Societally, this power dynamic shifts control away from citizens and toward massive tech conglomerates and state actors, leading to unprecedented levels of tracking and behavior manipulation. A prominent example is the widespread deployment of facial recognition technology by governments and law enforcement, which frequently misidentifies people of color and creates chilling effects on public dissent and the fundamental right to anonymity.

Furthermore, the widespread adoption of AI introduces the profound threat of widespread economic displacement and the proliferation of misinformation, reshaping the fabric of labor markets and public trust. As generative AI and sophisticated automation rapidly advance, they displace not only routine manufacturing tasks but also cognitive, creative, and white-collar professions, threatening to widen the wealth gap before new job sectors can emerge. Simultaneously, the weaponization of AI-generated content, such as highly convincing deepfakes and automated bot networks, makes it remarkably easy to manipulate public opinion and disrupt democratic processes. The societal fallout is already visible in elections worldwide, where deepfake audio and video clones of political figures are used to spread highly targeted disinformation, severely undermining institutional trust and democratic stability.
