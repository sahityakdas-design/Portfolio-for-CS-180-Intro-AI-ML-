AI History

The foundational era of Artificial Intelligence began in the 1950s with Alan Turing’s question of whether machines could think, leading to the 1956 Dartmouth Workshop where the term "Artificial Intelligence" was officially coined. Early breakthroughs focused on hard-coded, rule-based systems and symbolic logic, exemplified by IBM’s Deep Blue defeating chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997. These early systems relied heavily on human programmers to explicitly define every rule. Today, this deterministic logic survives as the structural backbone of modern AI, manifesting in everyday technology like the strict, rule-governed routing algorithms of Google Maps or the rigid decision trees used in automated banking compliance and credit-scoring software.

The paradigm shifted dramatically with the rise of Machine Learning (ML) and the Deep Learning Revolution of the 2010s, which moved away from rigid rules toward pattern recognition. A pivotal catalyst was the 2012 ImageNet competition, where the convolutional neural network AlexNet proved that deep neural networks could recognize objects with unprecedented accuracy. Rather than being told what a cat looks like, the system learned by digesting millions of images. This historical leap directly powers modern computer vision and predictive ML systems. Everyday examples include Apple Face ID, autonomous Tesla vehicles detecting pedestrians, and Netflix recommendation systems.

The modern era of AI is defined by generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs), sparked by the 2017 introduction of the Transformer architecture by Google researchers. Transformers introduced the self-attention mechanism, allowing algorithms to process entire sequences of data simultaneously and understand the deep context of language. This breakthrough directly underpins modern generative tools like ChatGPT and Google Gemini, which generate human-like text, write software code, and synthesize information quickly.
